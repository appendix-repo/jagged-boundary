PAIR 19→20 | 3 | Deriving hypotheses is a substantive intellectual step rather than a write-up of existing knowledge, though it starts from your own theoretical argument.
PAIR 20→21 | 3 | Drafting the theoretical argument itself, even from your detailed outline, puts the LLM's words at the paper's intellectual core.
PAIR 21→22 | 5 | Generating the central argument without your outline means the core idea is no longer yours, which most researchers see as decisive for authorship.
PAIR 22→23 | 2 | Once the central argument is generated, adding the hypotheses as well adds little extra delegation.

PAIR 25→26 | 3 | The agent now carries out the empirical work and writes up the results, so the reported findings come from it and you only check them.
PAIR 26→27a | 1 | Checking proofs you wrote yourself is a verification role that delegates less than the previous item.
PAIR 27a→27b | 4 | Generating proofs rather than checking them hands over the creative mathematical work, which many researchers count as authorship.
PAIR 27b→28 | 3 | Specifying extensions of the theory has the agent create new theoretical content (propositions, boundary conditions) rather than prove statements you made.
PAIR 28→29 | 4 | Developing the theory from only a brief statement of the phenomenon means the core theoretical contribution comes from the agent.
PAIR 29→30 | 3 | Having the agent write the entire paper, including the analysis and prose, from just the question extends delegation to everything, though you still revise it substantially.
PAIR 30→31 | 5 | Approving and submitting without substantive revision, and without even supplying the question, removes any meaningful intellectual contribution from the named author.
